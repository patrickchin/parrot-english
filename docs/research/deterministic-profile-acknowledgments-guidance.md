# Deterministic profile acknowledgments for beginner learners

Status: selected for implementation  
Research date: 2026-08-22  
Audience in scope: children around age five who may be pre-readers or Pre-A1
English learners, plus the grown-ups helping them

## Decision

After a profile answer has been saved, the child-facing confirmation will be the
exact phrase:

> Thank you!

The phrase will be selected locally by the product. It will not be generated by
the enrichment model and will not depend on the question, raw answer, saved
summary, canonical name or age, transcript, or prior stored acknowledgment.

This is a confirmation, not personalized teaching feedback. Peppa's happy
character state and the explicit **Next** action retain the friendly tone and
pacing without asking a model to improvise the largest text on the screen.

The implementation is intentionally split into two stacked improvements:

1. Make the public text deterministic and stop requesting acknowledgment prose
   from the enrichment model.
2. Reuse the existing saved `peppa-thank-you` audio so an answer no longer waits
   for runtime acknowledgment synthesis.

Keeping the changes separate makes the language/data boundary reviewable on its
own and lets the following timing change demonstrate its own provider and
latency reduction.

## Product question

The current profile flow asks a model to write a warm, playful acknowledgment
for every answer. The result can be up to 160 characters, is stored in the
profile, becomes the child screen's `h1`, and is sent verbatim to ElevenLabs.
Examples accepted by the current tests include:

- `Mia is a lovely name!`
- `Thirty is a brilliant age!`
- `Dinosaurs are very stompy!`

The JSON schema limits shape and length, but not reading level, subjective
judgment, invented words, personal-data echo, or reliable meaning. A failed or
unavailable model falls back to six hand-written phrases, but those still use
words and constructions such as `lovely`, `brilliant`, `exciting`, and `sounds
like`.

The product question is therefore: what is the smallest honest confirmation
that works for a very young learner and can be guaranteed for every possible
answer?

## Why `Thank you!`

`Thank you!` is two familiar words, one idea, and a conventional response to a
contribution. Cambridge includes it among Pre-A1 greetings and useful
expressions. It does not classify an answer as right or wrong, praise ordinary
profile facts as achievements, pretend the character has feelings about the
answer, or repeat potentially identifying or sensitive text aloud.

The phrase is also already paired with a checked-in ElevenLabs asset whose
visible and spoken text are both exactly `Thank you!`. That matters for the
stacked audio improvement: using the existing exact asset avoids a text/audio
mismatch and avoids generating substitute audio with an unapproved local or
system voice.

This is a product hypothesis, not proof that every five-year-old understands
the phrase. Pre-A1 is a proficiency level rather than an age, and children in
this audience will have uneven literacy and English exposure. The phrase must
remain paired with the happy character state, an immediately available Next
button, and direct observation with children and caregivers.

## Alternatives considered

### `Thank you! I have your answer.`

This is a stronger explicit receipt confirmation. Its vocabulary can be found
in the Cambridge Pre-A1 inventory, and its two short clauses still express one
point. It was not selected for this iteration because the younger edge of the
audience benefits from the lower reading burden, the existing saved audio says
only `Thank you!`, and the screen appears only after persistence succeeds.
Direct testing should compare the two phrases if children do not understand
the shorter state.

### One reviewed phrase per question

Examples such as `Nice to meet you!` or `Animals are fun!` remove stochastic
model output but still introduce different vocabulary, topical judgments, and
more copy to audit and record. A varied catalog also makes it easier for a
future edit to reintroduce answer-specific praise or reading-level drift.

### `Great job!`

A name, age, or preference is not a correct performance. Praise can give the
wrong mental model for a profile form.

### `I love that!`

This can endorse harmful or sensitive input and overstates the character's
relationship or feelings.

### `Answer saved!`

This is explicit but uses a passive, technical formulation. `save` appears at
the A2 Flyers level in the Cambridge word list rather than Pre-A1.

### Continue model generation with stricter prompting

Groq's structured output can guarantee that an output matches a supported JSON
schema. Its documentation distinguishes that guarantee from semantic
correctness, and seeded determinism is best effort. Prompting, an enum, or
post-generation filters would spend a model call to reproduce locally known
copy while providing a weaker boundary than a constant.

## Data and provider boundary

The selected contract is input-independent:

- Public confirmation text consumes no child answer or generated field.
- The model schema contains only the private enrichment fields needed for the
  factual summary and canonical name or age.
- New response snapshots retain the existing `acknowledgment` field for stored
  schema compatibility, but write the local constant into it.
- Old version-two snapshots remain readable. The API projects the current
  constant instead of returning a historical generated acknowledgment.
- Identical-answer retries reuse the saved factual enrichment while selecting
  current public copy locally.
- A save error produces no success acknowledgment.

This narrows one data path; it does not eliminate child-data processing. Raw
answers are still sent to Groq for the explicit summary/canonicalization
purpose, and profile/transcript collection, retention, consent, and deletion
remain separate privacy concerns.

## Timing baseline and expected change

Before these changes, a form answer can wait sequentially for:

1. Groq enrichment (15-second default request timeout, capped at 60 seconds).
2. Database persistence.
3. ElevenLabs acknowledgment synthesis (10-second default request timeout,
   capped at 30 seconds).

The child remains in the thinking state through that chain, including when
runtime synthesis eventually returns no audio. Deterministic text alone does
not remove the Groq enrichment wait. The stacked saved-audio improvement will
remove the runtime acknowledgment-synthesis request and its tail from this
critical path. No millisecond savings are claimed until measured; the reliable
structural claim is zero runtime TTS provider requests for this confirmation.

## Acceptance contract

The language-boundary implementation should prove that:

- generated-success, missing-key, refusal, invalid JSON, invalid schema,
  upstream error, and timeout enrichment paths use the same public text;
- the model prompt and response schema no longer request an acknowledgment;
- arbitrary Unicode, markup, personal data, unsafe content, and
  prompt-injection-like answers cannot alter the visible/spoken phrase;
- newly persisted snapshots contain exactly `Thank you!`;
- an identical-answer retry seeded with an old/private acknowledgment skips
  enrichment but returns the current constant;
- profile API reads sanitize historical acknowledgment text without rewriting
  or corrupting otherwise valid stored data;
- bulk edits preserve questionnaire order and return one deterministic
  acknowledgment only for each changed answer;
- canonical-name/age validation and atomic save failures still show no success
  acknowledgment;
- the existing 160-character component fixture remains as defensive legacy UI
  coverage, not as allowed current server output; and
- fixed-copy flows at narrow phone and short-landscape sizes retain heading
  focus, a visible 44-pixel Next target, header clearance, and no overflow.

The saved-audio follow-up should additionally prove that:

- response audio is the existing `/assets/audio/peppa-thank-you.mp3` asset with
  matching ID and text;
- the browser plays the static source directly without base64 decoding or
  object-URL allocation;
- the worker has no runtime acknowledgment TTS dependency, request, timeout,
  or production ElevenLabs secret requirement; and
- delayed or failed optional audio playback never blocks the visible Next
  action.

## Direct testing still needed

Observe children and caregivers rather than inferring comprehension from a
word list. Ask whether the child understands that Peppa received the answer,
can find Next without help, and stays comfortable through six repeated
confirmation screens. If repetition feels tedious, a later operation-level UX
change should test one confirmation after a multi-field profile edit instead of
cycling identical screens. That is separate from making each individual state
safe and predictable.

## Sources

- [Council of Europe, *CEFR Companion Volume with New Descriptors*
  (2020)](https://rm.coe.int/cefr-companion-volume-with-new-descriptors-2020/16809ea0d4)
  describes Pre-A1 use of a small repertoire of familiar words and formulaic
  expressions. It is a proficiency framework, not an age-five UI standard.
- [Cambridge English, *Pre A1 Starters, A1 Movers and A2 Flyers word list*
  (2025)](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/Images/739104-starters-movers-flyers-word-list-2025.pdf)
  provides the vocabulary-level check, including `thank you` at Pre-A1 and
  `save` at A2. The qualifications mainly target ages 6–12 and do not prove
  comprehension for every learner.
- [Cambridge English, *Pre A1 Starters Digital and A1 Movers Digital Handbook
  for Teachers*](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/latinamerica/Images/721650-pre-a1-starters-digital-and-a1-movers-digital-handbook-for-teachers.pdf)
  explicitly includes `Thank you` among Pre-A1 greetings and expressions.
- [W3C WAI, *Making Content Usable for People with Cognitive and Learning
  Disabilities* (2021)](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/design_guide.html)
  recommends common words, short literal sentences, simple structure, and one
  point at a time. It is an advisory Working Group Note, not normative WCAG.
- [W3C WAI, Technique G199 (updated
  2025)](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Techniques/general/G199) supports
  consistent success feedback after submitted data is accepted. It is not
  child-specific.
- [ICO Children's Code: transparency](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/4-transparency/)
  and [data minimisation](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/8-data-minimisation/)
  call for concise, age-appropriate information and minimum necessary data use.
  Avoiding public answer echo is a product inference, not quoted legal advice.
- [UNICEF, *Guidance on AI and Children 3.0* (December
  2025)](https://www.unicef.org/innocenti/media/11991/file/UNICEF-Innocenti-Guidance-on-AI-and-Children-3-2025.pdf)
  recommends privacy by design and purpose-specific, proportionate child-data
  processing. Choosing a constant instead of model transformation is an
  implementation inference.
- [Groq Structured Outputs](https://console.groq.com/docs/structured-outputs)
  and [Groq prompting guidance](https://console.groq.com/docs/prompting),
  accessed 2026-08-22, document schema guarantees and the limits of semantic
  accuracy and determinism.
- [FTC, *Complying with COPPA: Frequently Asked
  Questions*](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-coppa-frequently-asked-questions)
  notes that a child's voice recording can be personal information. Fixed
  confirmation copy does not resolve consent, retention, or provider-processing
  obligations.
