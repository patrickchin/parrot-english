# Beginner Language and Age

Last reviewed: 2026-08-21  
Question: How should Parrot English adapt language, tasks, and support for a
five-year-old beginner whose home language is not English, while remaining
useful to other young learners?

## Conclusion

Design from the learner's current language skill and support needs, then use age
for themes, motor demands, pacing, safeguarding, and adult involvement. "Age
five" is not a proficiency level. Parrot should begin with spoken language,
meaning-bearing pictures, demonstration, and answers as small as a tap, yes/no,
or one word. Reading can be optional support, not the only path to meaning.

The Council of Europe's young-learner descriptor sets cover ages 7–10 and
11–15, and explicitly are not a definitive age road map. There is too little
validated descriptor evidence to pretend that these sources precisely define
under-six capability. Product decisions for younger children therefore combine
Pre-A1 task evidence, developmental guidance, direct usability observation,
and conservative assumptions.

## Working language rules

### Child-facing directions

- Say one concrete action: "Tap the parrot," "Listen," or "Say: hello."
- Prefer a visible demonstration to another sentence of explanation.
- Keep the thing and action together: "Tap the red cup," not "Select the
  appropriate option below."
- Avoid metaphor, idiom, phrasal complexity, and unexplained school or app
  jargon.
- Use positive instructions. Say what to do next, not a list of mistakes to
  avoid.
- Treat 13 words for a direction and 7 words for a child-facing question as
  warning lights, not automatic quality gates. A shorter sentence can still be
  abstract or confusing.
- Read copy aloud during review. If punctuation or layout carries meaning the
  spoken line does not, revise it.

### Practice shape

Use a repeatable four-part loop:

1. **Model:** a character says the word or phrase while the matching meaning is
   visible.
2. **Join:** the learner can listen again and speak with the model.
3. **Try:** the learner gives a short independent response.
4. **Use again:** the same language appears in a slightly different picture or
   story moment.

Early success may be a point, choice, yes/no, or single word. A full sentence is
an invitation after meaning is secure, not the entry fee for progress.

### Pictures

Art must communicate the same proposition as the words. If the line is "The
pig is under the table," the pig, table, and under relationship should all be
clear. Decorative scenery must not compete with the target or contradict it.
Review at the actual child viewport, not only at source resolution.

### Feedback

- Confirm meaning or effort before correcting form.
- Give one usable next attempt: replay, slower model, first sound, or a shorter
  response.
- Never describe a young beginner as bad, wrong, lazy, behind, or failing.
- Do not require repeated speech to escape a screen. Preserve a safe Skip or
  grown-up-help route.

## Future audience modes

The core interaction can support more ages without mixing every need into one
screen:

| Mode | Likely emphasis | Must still be measured |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Early learner / approximately 4–6 | Listening, pictures, imitation, one-step actions, close adult support | Can the child understand and recover without reading? |
| Emerging reader / approximately 7–10 | Sound–word connection, short phrases, simple choices, more independent replay | Reading skill separately from speaking/listening skill |
| Older beginner / approximately 11–15 | Age-respectful themes, explicit goals, private retry, less babyish characters | Motivation and dignity without increasing language complexity too quickly |
| Any age with cognitive, language, motor, hearing, or vision support needs | Adjustable pace, modalities, target size, captions, replay, predictable sequence | Individual access needs rather than age proxies |

These are research hypotheses, not automatic age gates. A future placement flow
should be brief, reversible, skill-specific, and editable by a grown-up.

## Measurement

Prefer:

- first-attempt task comprehension without adult explanation;
- number and type of replays or help requests;
- independent recovery after a missed response;
- target-language recall or transfer in a later, different context;
- child affect and willingness to pause or finish, observed ethically;
- caregiver understanding of the activity's goal.

Do not optimize for session length, repeated tapping, or number of generated
lines. Those can increase while learning, agency, or well-being decreases.

## Evidence and limits

Primary references are [LANG-01 through LANG-08](./source-register.md). The
Cambridge handbook supplies Pre-A1 can-do and task examples, but it is an
assessment handbook. British Council sources are practitioner guidance. The
young-learner CEFR descriptors do not cover the product's youngest audience.
The next evidence step is moderated observation with children and caregivers,
not a more confident age rule.

## Open questions

- Which words and instructions are misunderstood by the current five-year-old
  audience when no adult translates them?
- Does a pictorial first-use demonstration outperform helper text?
- Which learners benefit from optional home-language support, and how can it be
  offered without making translation the only strategy?
- What is the smallest respectful adaptation that makes the same practice loop
  suitable for older beginners?
