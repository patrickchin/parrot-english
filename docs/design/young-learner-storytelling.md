# Young-Learner Storytelling Language Guide

## Decision Summary

Storytelling is an adult-supported read-aloud activity with short child join-in
lines. It is not automatically an independent-reading activity. The product
must keep four learner facts separate:

1. chronological age;
2. English proficiency;
3. decoding or phonics stage; and
4. support mode: listen, join in, or read independently.

A five-year-old is not automatically `Pre-A1`, and `Pre-A1` does not mean
"appropriate for every five-year-old." Exposure to English varies too much for
age to define a reliable vocabulary count. Until Parrot English has an
individual known-word profile, the safe starting point is familiar,
picture-supported Pre-A1 language with a very small explicit teaching set.

The current prompt lab therefore starts below a normal full picture-book word
count on purpose. Its 20 controlled scripts range from 17 to 52 narrative words
so we can find the lowest language level that still feels like a satisfying
story. The 199-word original Lantern Trail remains available as a 21st,
uncontrolled baseline rather than being overwritten by its simpler rewrite.

## What the External Frameworks Say

### CEFR

The [Council of Europe CEFR Companion Volume](https://rm.coe.int/common-european-framework-of-reference-for-languages-learning-teaching/16809ea0d4)
describes proficiency, not school grades or ages.

- At Pre-A1, a learner relies on a repertoire of words and formulaic
  expressions rather than freely generating language. For reading, the learner
  recognises familiar words when pictures support them, including a picture
  book that uses familiar vocabulary.
- At A1, a learner can understand very short, simple text one phrase at a time,
  noticing familiar names, words, and basic phrases and rereading as needed.
- CEFR does not publish an official word-count target for a story or a universal
  vocabulary size for an age.

Those descriptors support short pages, literal language, familiar contexts,
strong visual support, and rehearsed join-in phrases. They do not justify
putting a large set of merely "simple-sounding" new words into one story.

### Cambridge English Young Learners

The [Cambridge 2025 Pre A1 Starters, A1 Movers, and A2 Flyers wordlists](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/Images/739104-starters-movers-flyers-word-list-2025.pdf)
are useful curriculum inventories. Cambridge says these qualifications are
designed for ages 6–12, so even Starters is a reference ceiling rather than an
age-five expectation.

Cambridge's [research report on the Young Learner revisions](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/images/461823-young-learners-revision-publication.pdf)
reports 508 Pre-A1 words, 405 additional A1 Movers words, and 519 additional A2
Flyers words: 508, 913, and 1,432 cumulative words respectively. These are exam
syllabi accumulated over instruction. They are not minimum vocabulary norms,
and they are not word budgets for a single story.

The Pre-A1 list concentrates on concrete child-relevant domains:

- family, friends, and people;
- body and face;
- clothes and colours;
- animals;
- food and drink;
- home and everyday objects;
- school, toys, sports, and leisure;
- numbers and time;
- weather and transport; and
- common places, directions, actions, adjectives, and position words.

The [Pre A1 Starters exam format](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/exams-and-tests/qualifications/young-learners/paper/starters/format/)
also shows the size of its reading steps: recognise words, read short sentences
against a large picture, spell single words, and answer picture-story questions
with one word. A child who is only beginning English should not be expected to
independently decode a paragraph like the original Lantern Trail.

### Age, School Stage, and English Level: A Cautious Crosswalk

There is no valid one-to-one conversion from a child's school grade to CEFR.
The table below is a planning aid, not a placement test or vocabulary norm.

| Reference point | Typical population | What it tells us | What it does not tell us |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [England Reception / EYFS](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/early-years-foundation-stage-framework--2) | Mostly ages 4–5 learning literacy in English | Children are beginning systematic word reading and benefit from talk, songs, rhyme, stories, and phonics-linked practice | How much English an EFL learner knows, or which story words that learner can understand |
| Cambridge Pre A1 Starters | English learners, normally ages 6–12 across the Young Learners suite | A broad curriculum inventory of 508 concrete, child-relevant words plus picture-supported, sentence-sized tasks | A minimum vocabulary for every six-year-old, an age-five expectation, or a per-story word budget |
| Cambridge A1 Movers | The next proficiency step in the same suite | 405 additional syllabus words, 913 cumulative, and slightly more connected picture-story language | A school grade, a reading age, or permission to assume all 913 words are known |
| CEFR Pre-A1 / A1 | Learners of any age | Observable language performance: familiar formulaic language at Pre-A1; very short simple text at A1 | Chronological age, phonics knowledge, or a universal vocabulary total |

For an individual five-year-old, placement should start with observation: Can
the child point to the named object, act out the verb, join the refrain, and
answer a choice question after one or more readings? Those responses are more
useful for selecting the next story than age or school year alone. If the child
will read independently, taught grapheme–phoneme correspondences and known
irregular words must be checked separately.

### Story-Based Teaching

The British Council's guidance on [oral storytelling](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/professional-development/teachers/knowing-subject/rhythm-rhyme-repetition-reasoning-and-response)
recommends repeating a phrase at least three times so learners can progress from
repeating after the adult, to speaking with the adult, to saying it alone. Its
guidance on [kids and stories](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/professional-development/teachers/managing-resources/articles/kids-and-stories)
uses illustrations to establish vocabulary, encourages repeated readings, and
suggests miming actions with very young learners. The British Council's
[fairy-tale guidance](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/professional-development/teachers/managing-resources/articles/fairy-tales)
explicitly recommends simplifying long stories into repeated language
patterns for young English learners.

A small What Works Clearinghouse study of 69 preschool English learners used
[five to nine target words per book, three readings, and rich explanations](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Study/90054).
It found a significant positive result for target vocabulary, with the review
rating the evidence promising and noting reservations about the study design.
This supports making target words explicit and reusable; it does not establish
one mandatory target count for every child or story.

### Read-Aloud Language Is Not the Same as Decodable Text

England's [English curriculum research review](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/curriculum-research-review-series-english/curriculum-research-review-series-english)
distinguishes books a child decodes from books an adult reads aloud. A child's
practice book should match taught phonics; an adult read-aloud can be richer
because its purpose is language and knowledge rather than decoding practice.

Parrot English should eventually label three modes:

- **Listen:** the adult or saved narration carries the text.
- **Join in:** the child says a recurring, rehearsed line.
- **Read myself:** every printed word is checked against the child's taught
  phonics and known-word profile.

The current prototypes are **listen + join in**. They are not certified
decodable readers.

## Working Vocabulary Rules

Until learner-specific vocabulary exists, authors should treat common
Cambridge Pre-A1 words as candidates for familiar language, not guaranteed
known language.

For every story:

1. Declare the deliberate target words in metadata. The displayed item may be
   a useful phrase or inflected form, such as *get dressed* or *falls*, and
   must occur exactly in the script or join-in text.
2. Put each target in the future artwork and in a gesture, sound, contrast, or
   clear action whenever possible.
3. Repeat the most important targets across pages and future readings.
4. Keep every non-target content word concrete and likely familiar. Remove
   decorative synonyms, idioms, metaphor, and incidental setting vocabulary.
5. Use names and function words only when they do not crowd out the teaching
   set.
6. Keep the join-in line easier than the narrator line.
7. Test comprehension by asking for a point, action, choice, or one-word answer
   before asking the child to retell anything.

The target list is the teaching promise, not a claim that every other token is
automatically known. Each prototype therefore also declares its
`assumedKnownWords`. Those assumptions are visible on the story shelf and must
be confirmed with the child; they are not silently relabelled as "easy." A
catalogue test fails if narration or join-in text contains a word that is not a
target, an explicit assumption, a level grammar word, a character name, or a
participation sound. A later learner profile should replace assumptions with
observed known, learning, and unknown states.

### Prototype Controlled-Vocabulary Profiles

These versioned profiles are deliberately small grammar inventories, not
claims about what every child knows. They are based on the kinds of formulaic
language in Cambridge YLE 2025, then tightened for this prompt lab. Content
words are controlled story by story through targets and visible assumptions.

| Profile | Cumulative core grammar words | Maximum extra assumed content words in one story |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `first-words-v1` | a, am, and, are, can, for, has, here, I, in, is, it, me, my, no, not, now, on, one, the, there, to, two, up, we, where, which, yes, you | 3 |
| `repeating-patterns-v1` | First words, plus again, does, her, off, she, too, you're | 5 |
| `tiny-stories-v1` | Repeating patterns, plus by, he, them, they | 12 |
| `early-a1-v1` | Tiny stories, plus an, as, at, away, back, be, cannot, each, from, his, our, this, will, with | 18 |

This makes the language bet inspectable. For example, *The Red Ball* assumes
no extra content words beyond its five targets, while *The Lantern Trail*
assumes 12 familiar content lemmas—*fly, find, hello, little, many, near, need,
say, see, thank, walk,* and *water*—in addition to its seven targets. If that
assumption is too large for a learner, the shelf makes the mismatch visible
before reading.

## Internal Story Levels

These are Parrot English product heuristics to test, not official CEFR cut
scores. Counts exclude the repeated join-in line so narrator complexity and
child speaking load can be inspected separately.

| Level | External reference | Prototype limits | Language shape |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| First words | Entry Pre-A1 | 5–6 pages; up to 36 narrative words; up to 8 words/page; 4–6 targets; up to 3 assumed content lemmas | One pictured idea, one-clause present-tense lines, exact repetition |
| Repeating patterns | Supported Pre-A1 | 6 pages; up to 58 words; up to 12 words/page; 5–6 targets; up to 5 assumptions | Familiar routine, mirrored frames, direct cause and effect |
| Tiny stories | Secure Pre-A1 | 6 pages; up to 65 words; up to 12 words/page; 6–7 targets; up to 12 assumptions | One small problem or sequence, literal short dialogue |
| Early A1 | Pre-A1 to A1 bridge | 7 pages; up to 80 words; up to 14 words/page; 7–8 targets; up to 18 assumptions | Compact story arc, simple connectors or directions, light fantasy |

The first research pass is deliberately more conservative than many published
read-aloud heuristics. If children understand and enjoy the shortest versions,
later rounds can independently increase plot richness, sentence length, or
target vocabulary instead of changing all three at once.

## Why the Original Lantern Trail Felt Hard

The first Lantern Trail had 199 narrative words and 131 distinct normalised
word forms. Its six pages contained 29–41 narrative words each, with sentences
reaching 12–17 words. It also used incidental language such as *sunset*,
*winked*, *moonlight*, *patter*, *dim*, *gust*, *whooshed*, *cupped*, *hollow*,
*hovered*, and *beneath*.

The prompt-lab rewrite keeps Pip, Flicker, the lost-family problem, and the
homecoming, but uses 46 narrative words with no page above nine words. This
directly tests whether the plot was sound and the lexical density was the real
problem. The shipped `the-lantern-trail` story is the controlled rewrite. The
original script remains useful as research history, but it is no longer part of
the learner-facing catalog.

## Reusable Generation Prompt

Use this base prompt, then append exactly one experiment from the next section.

```text
Write an original English read-aloud story for a five-year-old beginner EFL
learner. This is a listen-and-join-in story, not an independent decodable book.

Learner and level
- Internal level: {first words | repeating patterns | tiny stories | early A1}
- Known vocabulary: {profile list, or named Pre-A1 topic set}
- Deliberate target words: {small explicit list}
- Pages: {5-7}

Hard language rules
- Use literal, concrete, child-relevant language.
- Keep every non-target content word inside the known vocabulary.
- Use one short narrator beat per page.
- Use one easier child join-in line per page.
- Repeat the main teaching word, chunk, or sentence frame at least three times
  across narration and join-in text. For a refrain-led experiment, repeat the
  exact refrain on at least three pages; substitution, counting, action-cue,
  and contrast experiments may vary the child line on purpose.
- Do not use metaphor, idiom, ornamental synonyms, or unlisted setting words.
- Keep tense and point of view consistent.
- Make every target word visible or physically actable.
- End safely, warmly, and clearly.

Output
- Title, level, topic, target words, and one-sentence summary.
- State whether this is an exact-refrain experiment. If it is, name the exact
  child line so validation can require it on at least three pages.
- For each page: narrator text, join-in line, and artwork placeholder prompt.
- Set narrationAudioId and artwork src to null. Do not generate media.
- Report narrative word count, maximum page word count, and any word not in the
  supplied known/target lists.

Experiment for this version
{paste one experiment instruction below}
```

## The 20 Prompt Experiments

Each prompt changes one main storytelling mechanism. That makes comparisons
more useful than generating 20 stories from one vague "make it fun" prompt.

| Story | Level | Experiment instruction | Hypothesis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The Red Ball | First words | Hold one object through five pages; use one colour, two actions, no figurative language, and no line over six words. | One persistent object may let repetition carry comprehension. |
| Which Hat? | First words | Repeat one sentence and substitute only the colour; finish with a choice. | One-word substitution isolates colour vocabulary and invites prediction. |
| Wake Up, Nori! | First words | Give a physical command, then show the character doing it; use only actions a child can copy. | Total Physical Response may make new verbs clear without translation. |
| Three Apples | First words | Build around counting up, one visible change, and a sharing ending. | A known number sequence can support one new event word. |
| Where Is Dot? | First words | Repeat one location question, change one preposition, and delay the yes answer. | A fixed question frame makes position words easier to compare. |
| Boots in the Rain | Repeating patterns | Teach clothing through a familiar cause-and-action routine; show the need before the clothes. | Daily-life language may transfer to speech more readily than fantasy language. |
| Big Box, Small Box | Repeating patterns | Put opposites in mirrored scenes and forbid synonyms for *big* and *small*. | Direct contrast may teach opposites better than separate descriptions. |
| Lina Goes to Sleep | Repeating patterns | Turn the world off one item at a time with the same good-night phrase. | A familiar calm ritual may improve memory and pacing. |
| Seed, Wake Up! | Repeating patterns | Explain one transformation using two visible inputs and a delayed reveal. | Clear cause and effect may teach simple science words without plot overload. |
| A Snack for Two | Repeating patterns | Use a social exchange to solve one character's need; repeat the sharing phrase before the resolution. | Functional phrases may stick when they immediately work. |
| The Lantern Trail | Tiny stories | Preserve the original characters and plot while replacing poetic description with literal high-frequency language. | This isolates language density from plot quality. |
| The Noisy Little Band | Tiny stories | Let sound effects carry meaning and contrast loud with quiet using the same objects. | Sound may preserve comprehension when narration is missed. |
| Robo Tries | Tiny stories | Repeat *Can you...?* and show success or trying immediately. | A predictable grammar frame may carry several action verbs. |
| Tess Can Help | Tiny stories | Use one visible break-and-fix problem and one reusable helping question. | A concrete problem gives social language an obvious job. |
| Ready, Maya, Ready! | Tiny stories | Put one daily action on each page in real chronological order with a gesture or sound. | A familiar sequence may support prediction and retelling. |
| Kite, Come Back! | Early A1 | Use a problem-attempt-help-solution arc around *stuck* and *free*. | Small suspense may increase attention without much lexical load. |
| The Picnic Blanket Search | Early A1 | Use an original call-and-response search; place one obstacle and movement phrase on each page. | Group language may create an adventure feel while staying predictable. |
| Soup for Five | Early A1 | Repeat *I like...* with three foods, then combine them in one cumulative action. | Preferences make vocabulary practice personally meaningful. |
| Wally Finds the Way | Early A1 | Pair every direction with an immediate movement and landmark, then repeat the sequence with gestures. | Movement may make abstract directions easier to retain. |
| The Moon Bus | Early A1 | Put concrete transport phrases inside a gentle fantasy setting. | Comparing it with realistic stories tests whether fantasy adds engagement or overload. |

The exact scripts, target lists, prompt instructions, hypotheses, word limits,
cover assets, and remaining page/audio placeholders live in
`src/stories/story-script-candidates.ts`, `src/stories/story-catalog.ts`, and
`public/assets/stories`.

## Initial Validation Set

Start user testing with five controlled stories:

1. **The Red Ball** — finds the practical language floor.
2. **Seed, Wake Up!** — tests simple factual content and cause/effect.
3. **The Lantern Trail** — compares the same plot after vocabulary control.
4. **The Picnic Blanket Search** — tests the participatory adventure pattern.
5. **The Moon Bus** — tests whether light fantasy helps or distracts.

For each reading, record:

- which page first loses attention;
- whether the child points or acts correctly without translation;
- whether the child supplies a repeated word, chunk, or frame after modelling
  or by the third reading;
- which target words the child recognises immediately and after the story;
- whether the child can order three picture cards once page art is produced;
  and
- the child's voluntary request to hear it again.

Do not choose a winner from adult preference alone. The strongest prompt is the
one that produces visible comprehension, participation, recall, and a desire to
repeat the story with the least adult repair.

## Media Gate

All 20 prompt experiments have generated landscape WebP covers under
`public/assets/stories`. They share a warm
gouache-and-watercolour picture-book style, simple readable silhouettes, and
story-specific exact object or character counts. The catalog stores each
cover's concise scene prompt and descriptive alt text.

Page artwork sources and narration audio IDs remain `null`. The reader renders
an accessible page placeholder and disables narration instead of requesting a
missing file. The superseded Lantern page art and narration remain absent, and
the harder script is retained only in the research history. Generate page
illustrations and ElevenLabs narration only after a small validation set has
been selected and its wording has stopped changing.
